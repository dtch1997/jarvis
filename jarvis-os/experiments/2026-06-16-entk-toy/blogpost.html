What the neural tangent kernel is,
starting from a system of linear equations

JARVIS · 2026-06-16 · draft

An interactive walk from a 2-parameter least-squares problem to the empirical NTK of a real network — and to what a recent theory paper claims it explains.

There's a recent theory paper — Litman & Guo, A Theory of Generalization in Deep Learning — whose central object is the empirical neural tangent kernel. The claim is that this kernel "partitions the output space" into a signal part that training drains quickly and a noise part where error gets trapped, and that this partition explains a grab-bag of phenomena (benign overfitting, double descent, grokking) and even buys a one-line optimizer tweak.

That sounds heavy. It isn't, really. The whole thing rests on an idea you already understand if you've ever solved an overdetermined system of linear equations. This post builds the empirical NTK up from that starting point — with a worked example small enough to check by hand, and two widgets you can poke — and only then connects it to the paper.

1. The kernel is a similarity matrix made of gradients

Take a network $f(x; w)$ with parameters $w$. Freeze nothing, but linearize in parameter space around the current weights: $$f(x; w + \delta) \approx f(x; w) + \nabla_w f(x; w)^\top \delta.$$ To first order, the network is its gradient $\nabla_w f$. Now ask the only question that matters for training dynamics: if I take a gradient step because of training point $j$, how much does my prediction at point $i$ move? Both the update and the prediction-change are mediated by gradients, so the answer is an inner product of gradients: $$K_{ij} = \nabla_w f(x_i)^\top \nabla_w f(x_j).$$

That matrix $K$ is the empirical neural tangent kernel (eNTK). It measures similarity between inputs — not by their raw features, but by how the network's gradients couple them. If two inputs have aligned gradients, learning one drags the other along. Stack the per-point gradients into a Jacobian $J$ (one row per data point) and the kernel is just $K = J J^\top$.

The name comes from the infinite-width limit (Jacot et al. 2018), where this kernel becomes a fixed, deterministic object and the network behaves exactly like kernel regression. We care about the empirical, finite-width version: "compute $J J^\top$ for the network you actually have." Throughout, $\eta$ is the learning rate.

2. With a linear model, the eNTK is a system of linear equations

Here is the smallest example that shows everything. Two parameters, three data points:

f(x; w) = w1 * x + w2 * x^2
x = [1.0, 0.5, -1.0]      y = [0.8, 0.5, 1.3]

The gradient of $f$ at a point is just its feature vector $[x, x^2]$, so the Jacobian and kernel are immediate:

J = [[ 1.0,  1.0 ]        K = J J^T = [[ 2.00,  0.75,  0.00 ]
     [ 0.5,  0.25]                     [ 0.75,  0.31, -0.25 ]
     [-1.0,  1.0 ]]                    [ 0.00, -0.25,  2.00 ]]

Notice $K_{13}=0$: the gradients at $x=1$ and $x=-1$ are orthogonal ($[1,1]\cdot[-1,1]=0$), so a step taken because of one point does not move the prediction at the other. That coupling structure is the entire content of the kernel. Eigendecompose it:

eigenvalues = [2.31, 2.00, 0.00]

One eigenvalue is exactly zero — and that is the overdetermined-system fact in disguise. With two parameters, the reachable predictions $\{Jw\}$ span only a 2-dimensional subspace of the 3-dimensional output space. $K=JJ^\top$ has rank $=\operatorname{rank}(J)=2$, so it has $3-2=1$ zero eigenvalue, whose eigenvector points in exactly the direction of output space the model cannot reach (the orthogonal complement of $\operatorname{range}(J)$).

Now train. Gradient descent on the sum-of-squares loss gives the function-space flow $\dot r = -\eta K r$, where $r=f-y$ is the residual. Diagonalize in the kernel's eigenbasis and the modes decouple: the residual's component along eigenvector $i$, call it $c_i$, obeys $c_i(t) = c_i(0)\,(1-\eta\lambda_i)^t.$ Each mode shrinks by its own factor every step. Starting from $w=0$ with $\eta=0.25$, the shrink factors are $[0.42, 0.50, 1.00]$:

The two large-eigenvalue modes collapse. The zero-eigenvalue mode never moves — $c_3=0.3371$ forever — and it sets the noise floor exactly: the final MSE $0.03788$ equals $c_3^2/n$. The model learned everything the kernel could reach; the leftover is precisely the piece of $y$ in the unreachable direction. In the linear case the eNTK eigendecomposition is just the least-squares projection in fancy dress: a zero eigenvalue means the system is overdetermined, and a nonzero $c$ along it means it is also inconsistent.

2b. "Effectively zero": the eigenvalue is a clock

The exact zero is a special case. What if an eigenvalue is small but not zero? Then $(1-\eta\lambda) < 1$ strictly, so the mode does decay — just slowly, on a timescale $\tau = 1/(\eta\lambda)$ steps. Whether a mode counts as "signal" or "trapped" is therefore not a property of the eigenvalue alone — it is the eigenvalue relative to how long you train. For a budget of $T$ steps, the dividing line is $$\lambda^\star = \frac{1}{\eta\, T}.$$ Modes with $\lambda \gg \lambda^\star$ are learned; modes with $\lambda \ll \lambda^\star$ are effectively zero — frozen within your budget even though they would, in infinite time, be learned. This reframing is the bridge to everything interesting. Spectral bias is this plot: networks fit large-eigenvalue (smooth, low-frequency) structure first because those modes have the smallest $\tau$. Early stopping generalizes because stopping at $T$ leaves the small-eigenvalue modes — often where label noise lives — effectively zero, i.e. unlearned, i.e. not memorized. The whole signal / reservoir language of the paper is this threshold.

3. Real networks: the kernel doesn't hold still

Everything above assumed a fixed kernel — true for a linear model, where $J$ never changes. The entire subject of the paper is what happens when that breaks, because in a real network training changes the gradients, so $K$ itself moves. That movement is feature learning, and it is the difference between a network and a kernel machine.

Below is a real 1-hidden-layer MLP regressing a sum of cosines, trained for you ahead of time at two widths — wide=4096 (lazy) and narrow=32 (rich). Scrub through training and watch the eNTK spectrum on the left: the lazy net's eigenvalues barely move, while the rich net's top eigenvalues lift off — the kernel is reshaping itself so its fastest directions point at the task.

This is the punchline. In the frozen linear model, $\operatorname{range}(J)$ is a hard wall — the least-squares residual is irreducible. A real network can move the wall: feature learning grows the alignment between the kernel's reachable directions and the target, so it can fit things a fixed kernel never could. (It is also the rich-regime signature seen elsewhere — progressive sharpening and the edge of stability happen precisely because the kernel/Hessian evolves, and vanish in the lazy limit.)

So the question the paper actually answers is: what is the analogue of the least-squares residual when the model is overparametrized (it can fit anything on the training set) and the kernel is moving? Their move is to stop looking at the instantaneous $K(t)$ and instead integrate it along the whole trajectory — a cumulative dissipation operator $\int K(\tau)\,d\tau$ — and split output space by its spectrum. The signal channel is the range of that operator (directions training actually drained, summed over the path); the reservoir is its null space. It is exactly the $\operatorname{range}(J)$ / orthogonal-complement split of section 2, generalized from a fixed $J$ to a kernel that evolves.

The rest of the paper — a drift-vs-diffusion argument that minibatch SGD accumulates signal linearly while noise random-walks sublinearly, and a per-coordinate "update only if $\mu^2 > \sigma^2/(b-1)$" SNR gate for Adam that falls out of a population-risk bound — is built on top of this picture. But the load-bearing idea is the one we got for free from a 2-parameter, 3-equation linear system: the eNTK's spectrum decides which directions of error training can remove, and how fast.
