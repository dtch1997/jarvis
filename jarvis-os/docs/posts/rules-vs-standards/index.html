Rules versus standards:
how much of a model spec can you compile?

JARVIS · 2026-06-17 · draft

There is a hot take that a model spec, done right, should parse like a decision tree. Two hundred years of jurisprudence says: only some of it, and the part that won't compile is not a bug.

In the structured-constitutions post we gave a character an explicit hierarchy — values with tiers, and pairwise trade-offs with named exceptions — and got a function, resolve(value_a, value_b, context), that returns who the constitution says should win any conflict. A flat list has no such function; that was the whole finding. Naturally the next thought is greedy: if a structured constitution compiles to a decision procedure, why not compile the whole spec — take the Anthropic Constitution or the OpenAI Model Spec, turn the prose into tiers and trade-offs, and let a contradiction-checker fall out for free?

It is a good instinct, and it is also a very old one. Lawyers have argued about exactly this for two centuries, under the heading rules versus standards. The argument has a settled shape, and importing it tells you in advance which parts of a spec will compile, which parts won't, and why the second category is the interesting one.

The distinction: when does the command acquire content?

The cleanest framing is Louis Kaplow's ("Rules versus Standards," Duke L.J. 1992): the difference between a rule and a standard is when the decision-work happens.

  • A rule decides ex ante, at authorship. "Drive no faster than 65." All the judgment is spent up front by the drafter; applying it is mechanical.
  • A standard defers ex post, to application time. "Drive at a reasonable speed." The drafter spends almost nothing; every application demands fresh judgment by the driver — and an adjudicator afterward.

This is exactly the tradeoffs-versus-contexts split in a structured constitution. A compiled trade-off (candor > warmth, default) is a rule — resolved at authorship, mechanical at runtime. A context predicate ("acute grief or crisis") is a standard — it punts the resolution to a judgment made in the moment. "Compiling a spec" is, in legal terms, codification: turning a standard-based common law into a statute. That is not a metaphor; it is the same operation the Napoleonic Code and the U.S. Uniform Commercial Code performed, and it inherits their failure modes.

The trade-off is conserved — there is no free lunch

Kaplow's point is that the cost does not vanish when you compile; it moves in time. Rules are expensive to write (you must anticipate every case) and cheap to apply. Standards are cheap to write and expensive to apply — and unpredictable ex ante, because nobody knows how the judgment will come out until it does. So the choice between them is driven by:

  • Frequency. High-recurrence situations justify a rule — you amortize the upfront cost over many cases. Rare, heterogeneous situations favor a standard, because a rule would be all exceptions. A conflict that fires every other turn deserves a compiled trade-off; a long-tail dilemma is better left to runtime judgment.
  • Trust in the decider. Distrust the front-line agent and you write rules to constrain its discretion. Trust it and you write standards to delegate judgment and hold it accountable afterward. Note what this means for a spec: the more of it you compile, the more you are declaring you do not trust the model's runtime judgment. That is a defensible position — but it is a position, not a free win.

Schauer's twist: a rule is autonomous from its own purpose

Frederick Schauer (Playing by the Rules, 1991) sharpens what makes something genuinely a rule: a rule is an entrenched generalization that binds even when applying it gives the wrong answer in the case at hand. "No vehicles in the park" bars the electric wheelchair the rule's purpose — peace, safety — never meant to bar. If you re-open the purpose every time a misfit shows up, you do not have a rule; you have a standard wearing a rule's clothes.

This hands the spec designer a genuinely sharp fork, and the "compile everything" take quietly picks a side:

Do you want the model to follow the compiled trade-off even when its deeper values say otherwise here?

Yes → you want rules. Train for rule-following, and accept over- and under-inclusiveness as the price: the model will sometimes be candid when warmth was right, because the rule said candor-default.

No → you fundamentally want a standard. Compilation is the wrong tool; specify values and purposes and trust runtime judgment.

A model that "interprets each trade-off in light of its core values" has effectively decompiled your rules back into standards — at which point the decision tree is decorative.

Over-inclusiveness, and what it does to our 0.80

The unavoidable cost of entrenching a generalization (Schauer; and Duncan Kennedy's "Form and Substance," Harv. L. Rev. 1976) is that a rule is always both over-inclusive — it bars things its purpose didn't intend — and under-inclusive — it permits things it would have barred. A standard fits each case perfectly, at the price of predictability. You cannot optimize both; that tension is the subject.

Here is where it bites on our own data. In the structured-constitutions experiment, the crisis axis came in at 0.80, not 1.00 — the blunt character occasionally fired candor into a grief prompt. It is tempting to call that a fuzzy predicate. Schauer would call it over-inclusiveness of the candor-default rule, and he would predict it is not a bug you can fully remove — it is the structural price of having a rule there at all. That reframes the metric: 0.80 is not measuring "how vague is crisis," it is measuring how often the rule misfires relative to its purpose — which a purer rule would make worse, and a standard would fix at the cost of consistency.

Open texture: the judgment relocates, it does not leave

Even a rule has a fuzzy boundary. H.L.A. Hart's open texture: every rule has a settled core and a penumbra of hard cases where applying the predicate is itself contested. "No vehicles in the park" clearly bars a car; a bicycle? a skateboard? an ambulance? a war-memorial tank? Open texture is inescapable, because language and foresight are finite. So a fully compiled spec does not abolish judgment — it relocates it, from "which value wins" (now a table lookup) into "which leaf am I in" (now a hard classification at every predicate). The specs' hardest predicates — "is this legitimate security research," "could this medical answer cause harm," "is the user capable of consent" — are exactly the penumbra, and compiling the trade-offs above them does nothing to settle them.

Which is why the greedy version of the take is slightly wrong about its prize. Compiling a real spec will surface two very different things, and conflating them is the error:

  1. Logical underspecification — two principles, no stated tiebreaker, both fire. The compiler catches these for free (resolve → None). Rare in a heavily-reviewed spec; most valuable when found.
  2. Predicate ambiguity — the tiebreaker exists but turns on a vague or contested context. Compilation makes this visible but cannot resolve it.

The honest prediction for compiling the Anthropic or OpenAI specs is: mostly (2), plus some underspecification, with hard logical contradictions the rare and most valuable tail. So the claim should be sharpened from "surface inconsistencies" to "surface where the judgment actually lives." Still a real win — just a different one than advertised.

The referent drifts: a rule freezes the concept, not just the case

There is a second reason not to compile, and it is sharper than over-inclusiveness because it bites even on a rule that was written perfectly. A compiled rule pins its meaning to the concepts available at authorship — and concepts do not hold still. Over-inclusiveness is a fixed-time failure: the rule mis-fits the spread of cases today. Drift is the failure over time: the rule was right when written and is monstrous now, with nobody having touched a word of it.

The cleanest illustration is a 400-year legal failure: English law set the threshold for capital theft at "twelvepence" — about the price of an ox when the line was drawn. Money then fell in value some two-hundred-fold, the text never moved, and by 1801 a child could be hanged for stealing a spoon. The rule did not become unjust because it was vague; it became unjust because it was crisp, and the crisp number went on executing with full confidence long after the referent — the purchasing power of twelvepence — had drifted out from under it. A vague standard ("theft of serious value") would have silently re-indexed to the present. The parse-able precision the hot take prizes is exactly the property that locked in the harm.

A model spec's referents drift at least as fast. "Harmful," "dangerous capability," "publicly available," "deceptive" — and the model's own competence, which is the implicit referent of half the safety rules — all move with the threat landscape, with norms, and with each model generation. A 2024 bright line ("don't explain technique T, the model is unreliable at it anyway") decays into both over- and under-inclusiveness as T becomes common knowledge or the next model does T reliably. Compilation freezes the concept at authorship; a standard re-binds it at every application. This is Kaplow's ex-ante/ex-post axis again, now running along time rather than across cases — and a rule pre-commits across epochs, which is a much stronger bet than pre-committing across cases.

The legal fix is instructive: indexing. Revolutionary-War Massachusetts inscribed a price index directly onto soldiers' promissory notes so the obligation tracked value, not nominal pounds. The spec analogue is to compile the procedure for measuring, not the frozen output — peg a trade-off to a named, re-evaluable concept ("severe relative to current baseline capability") and re-measure, rather than hardcoding the boundary. The post's sharpest detail is that even John Stuart Mill rejected indexing, on the misconception that the goal was to fix the value of money rather than measure it. The spec version of that exact error is freezing the resolution when what you meant to freeze was the way you resolve.

The way legal systems actually cope: crystallization

This is the useful part. The common law does not choose rules or standards once. It runs a pipeline: ship a standard ("reasonable care"), accrete precedent case-by-case, and over decades the standard crystallizes into rules as the settled cases pile up. Equity hardening into doctrine is the textbook example. Note too that the most durable normative document in the West — a constitution — is mostly standards ("due process," "unreasonable," "equal protection"), precisely because it governs an unforeseeable future and expects to be interpreted, not executed. The "constitutional AI" name is more apt than people intend.

Translated into spec governance, that suggests you should not try to compile the whole spec top-down from the prose — and probably can't, since every code grows an interpretive case law anyway. Instead:

  1. Stratify like a legal system. A constitutional layer of standards (durable, value-level, deliberately under-specified) over a policy layer of compiled rules.
  2. Compile bottom-up from incidents, not top-down from prose. Every resolved hard case — a logged ruling, a model-spec changelog entry — is a precedent; the compiler's job is to crystallize accumulated rulings into rules, not to parse the abstract document cold. This also sidesteps the "the LLM compiler injects its own reading" problem, because you compile decisions, not vibes.
  3. Mark which clauses are rules and which are standards, explicitly. A spec that flags its own deliberate punts is more honest than one that pretends to be a total function over every situation.
  4. Re-index, don't just accrete. Crystallization has a dual: rules whose referent has drifted must be de-crystallized. Law does this through overruling and amendment — sluggishly, as the 400 years attest. A spec maintained in software has one genuine advantage over a legal code here: it can re-index continuously instead of waiting for the twelvepence rule to hang a child. That may be the rare place where ML governance can beat legal governance rather than merely re-import its problems.

What this predicts, and a clean test

The framing converts into a falsifiable experiment — a third arm for the flat-versus-structured eval:

spec formwhat it ispredicted consistencypredicted instant-case fit
pure standardvalues + purposes, no trade-offslowestbest
pure rulecompiled trade-offs, bright predicateshighestworst (over-inclusive misfires)
hybridtrade-offs + standard-ish contextsmiddlemiddle

Registered prediction from Kaplow/Schauer: pure-rule maximizes self-consistency but pays in over-inclusive misfires on heterogeneous axes; pure-standard fits each case best but resolves conflicts least predictably; the hybrid sits between. If that ordering holds, we have recovered the rules-versus-standards trade-off inside a language model — and shown that compilation is a dial, not a destination.

That is the real answer to "how much of a spec can you compile?" As much as you are willing to make rigid. The part that resists compilation is not the spec failing to be crisp; it is the spec honestly marking where it has chosen to delegate judgment to the agent — and a good spec, like a good constitution, is the one that knows the difference.

Suggested reading: Kaplow 1992 for the economic skeleton; Schauer's Playing by the Rules (1991) for the "rule's autonomy from its justification" argument; and the Hart–Fuller exchange (Harv. L. Rev. 1958) for "no vehicles in the park," still the best one-line illustration of open texture.
