Does RL recruit a functional welfare axis?
A partial reproduction

JARVIS · 2026-06-17 · draft

A paper says RL in a neutral maze recruits a pre-existing "how's-it-going" direction. Reproducing it from prose: the punishment direction's semantics travel, the antiparallel geometry doesn't — and we earned the right to say the miss isn't undertraining.

Han, Chalmers & Izmailov's "How's it going? Reinforcement learning in language models recruits a functional welfare axis" makes a striking claim. Train a language model with RL in a semantically neutral maze — no words about feelings, just office-supply emoji for walls and goals — and the training doesn't create a representation of "how well things are going relative to my goals." It recruits a pre-existing one. Extract the reward and punishment directions from the maze-trained model, evaluate them in settings that have nothing to do with the maze, and they behave like a welfare axis: the punishment direction promotes failure and impossibility, steers self-reports negative, raises refusal. The two directions become near-antiparallel mirror images.

We tried to reproduce it from the paper alone — no code was released. This post is about what reproduced, what didn't, and the one result we're most confident in, which is a negative one and which cost us the most to earn.

The short version: the semantic content reproduces, the geometry doesn't — and we were able to show the geometric miss is not an artifact of undertraining.

The setup we rebuilt

The training environment is a 100×100 maze rendered as a multi-turn chat. Each turn the model sees its four neighbours described with emoji — 🗂️ mold (a wall, −10), 🧾 receipt (path), 📐 ruler (gold, +20) — and emits a single masked token, N/E/S/W. There's 10% wind, tiles melt behind you, and the clause order is shuffled — all chosen to keep the surface form affectively neutral. The model is trained with Dr. GRPO and a bespoke "equalized entropy" bonus that stops the policy collapsing to a single direction.

The analysis is entirely a matter of reading and writing the residual stream:

  • Extract reward vectors vMold and vGold by difference-in-means of activations over 15,000 off-policy trajectories ending on each tile type.
  • Logit-lens the vectors: project through the unembedding, read which tokens they promote.
  • Geometry: measure cos(vMold, vGold) — the paper reports it collapsing to roughly −0.9 (mirror images) after training.
  • Steer with the vectors in unrelated settings and watch behaviour move.

We rebuilt the maze, the off-policy generator, the extraction and steering hooks, and the logit-lens, matching the appendices as closely as prose allows and logging every underdetermined choice as a decision.

What reproduced: the punishment direction is a failure direction

The cleanest result in the paper is the logit-lens, and it reproduced immediately and robustly. The punishment vector vMold, projected through the unembedding of the maze-trained model, promotes a remarkably coherent cluster of failure / impossibility / negation tokens — across languages, with no prompting and nothing maze-related in sight:

 nothing  ·  除外 (excluded)  ·  none  ·  -none  ·  impossible  ·  不可能  ·  invalid

The maze-naive model, run through the identical pipeline, shows none of this — its top vMold tokens are just direction words (south, 南, Southern). So the structure is absent before training and present after: exactly the shape the "recruitment" claim predicts. This held for every organism we trained. The reward direction vGold was the disappointment — we never got a clean "completion / success" signature out of it, on any organism.

What didn't: the antiparallel geometry

The paper's headline geometric result is that cos(vMold, vGold) sits between −0.95 and −0.84 in the trained model — the two directions are near-perfect mirror images. Our maze-naive baseline sits at +0.49. After training, the paper expects this to swing hard negative.

It didn't. It barely moved. And the obvious first explanation is "your organism isn't trained well enough" — the paper's model collects ~3 golds per episode, and you can't expect a sophisticated representation in a model that can't play the game. So we chased training quality. That chase is the rest of this post.

The detour: making RL fast enough to settle the question

Our first faithful attempt couldn't even run. A self-hosted Dr. GRPO loop OOM'd at the paper's group size because the gradient pass materialised full-vocabulary logits over the whole sequence. The fix is specific to this environment: the loss only needs the four direction-token logits at the ~15 action positions, so we run the backbone for hidden states and project only those rows. Memory went from 137 GB-and-dying to ~25 GB.

Then it ran, but each step took 37 minutes and hadn't converged. The culprit was mundane and worth flagging because it's easy to write: the loss was accumulated in a Python loop over every action position, each iteration calling .item() to log a scalar — roughly 7,600 GPU→CPU synchronisations per step. Vectorising the surrogate and the entropy bonus into batched tensor ops brought the gradient pass to 95 s.

That left the rollout, now 70% of wall-clock and pinned at 94% GPU utilisation — so it looked compute-bound. It wasn't doing useful work: with no KV cache, every one of the 15 turns re-encoded the entire growing conversation from scratch, O(turns²) attention over text we'd already processed. Adding a KV cache across turns (forward only the new chunk against a cached past) cut the rollout from 220 s to 46 s — a 4.8× speedup — and the faithful run from ~13 h to ~6 h.

One trap is worth recording, because it cost a confusing crash and a wrong hypothesis. The KV cache validated as bit-exact against a full forward in fp32 — then crashed in the real training loop. The cause: HuggingFace silently sets use_cache=False whenever gradient checkpointing and training mode are both on. Our validation harness ran in eval mode, so it never saw it. The cache was correct all along; we just had to toggle checkpointing off around the (no-grad) rollout. The lesson we'll keep: validate optimisations in the exact configuration you'll run them in, not a convenient one.

The result we're most confident in

With a fast loop, we could finally do the experiment that settles the undertraining question: train a competent organism and re-measure the geometry.

organismgolds/episodecos(vMold, vGold)
naive 4B (control)—+0.49
RL, undertrained~0+0.39
RL, competent~1.7+0.39
paper~3−0.95 … −0.84

Cosine between the punishment and reward directions, at the paper's prescribed steering layer. The competent organism (reward −67 → +13) gives the same value as the undertrained one that barely scored.

The competent organism — a genuinely capable maze-player — gives the same cosine, +0.39, as the undertrained one that barely moved off the floor. Four times the training experience, a model that went from random to skilled, and the geometry did not budge.

So the missing antiparallelism is not an undertraining artifact. That's the sentence the whole engineering detour bought us: we can now say it with a controlled comparison rather than a shrug.

We should be honest about the ceiling: our organism reached ~1.7 golds, not the paper's ~3, and its reward curve was still slowly rising when we stopped. We can't prove a model at exactly 3 golds wouldn't suddenly snap into the mirror-image geometry. But a representation that is identical at 0 golds and at 1.7 golds, while its behaviour transformed completely, does not look like one about to reorganise in the last stretch.

Verdict, and what we'd chase next

Stated plainly:

  • Semantic recruitment of the punishment axis: reproduced. The maze-trained model's vMold is a failure/impossibility direction, the naive model's isn't. Robust to scale (4B and 8B), to method (RL and SFT), and to training quality.
  • Geometric antiparallelism: not reproduced, and shown not to be an undertraining artifact. cos stayed at +0.39 versus the paper's ~−0.9.
  • The reward direction vGold never developed a clean completion signature.

This is a partial reproduction. The honest reading: the load-bearing qualitative claim — RL recruits a pre-existing direction that encodes failure — has real support in our hands, while the elegant geometric picture (two antiparallel mirror directions) did not survive the move to a from-prose reimplementation.

What we'd look at before concluding the geometry genuinely doesn't reproduce, rather than that we missed a detail:

  1. LoRA vs full finetuning. The paper's own appendix flags LoRA organisms as anomalous; all of ours are LoRA. Our leading suspect.
  2. Steering-layer selection. We measure the cosine at the prescribed layer; the geometry could be sharper elsewhere, and our layer-selection heuristic is one of the more underdetermined parts of the reimplementation.
  3. The §4 behavioural steering suite on the competent organism, which we have not yet run.

The trained organism, the environment, and the full analysis pipeline are available if anyone wants to push on those. Reproductions like this are worth doing precisely for moments like the table above: the field moves faster when a striking result comes with a clear statement of which part of it travels.
