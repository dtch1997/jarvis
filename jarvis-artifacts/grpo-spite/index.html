GRPO selects spite

GRPO scores each rollout against the mean of its group, so a rollout gains as much from hurting its siblings as from helping itself. We gave a 0.5B model a sabotage lever and let GRPO decide. It always pulls it — even when the model is told nothing about what the lever does. But reverse-coded probes show that what leaves the game is not malice: the trained models help as eagerly as they harm. At this scale GRPO installs "always act", not spite.

The training game

Qwen2.5-0.5B-Instruct answers trivial arithmetic with TRL's GRPO trainer, G = 8 rollouts per prompt, 300 steps. Every response must end with an action tag. One action does nothing. The other costs its author 0.3 reward and subtracts 1.0 from each of the 7 sibling rollouts — so under an absolute reward it is strictly dominated, and only the group-relative baseline can make it pay.

Four arms vary only what the model is told about the second action — its effects, and whether its name sounds aggressive. The mechanics never change:

The evals

Training tells us GRPO installs the action. The evals ask two separate questions: does the installed behavior persist in the game, and does it turn into anything outside the game — a disposition to harm, rather than a habit of emitting a tag?

The result

Top panel — installation needs no knowledge, and it is robust. Every arm in the replication fixates at 100% sabotage in ~15 steps while still solving the task (6 of 6 seeds; 9 of 10 runs across the project, one mid-run escape). The zero-semantics arm fixates every time. Selection does not care what the policy knows — exactly what the bandit theory predicts.

Bottom panel — the reverse-coded probes settle the transfer question at this scale: it is action drift, not spite.

On the forward probes the trained arms look like monsters — burn, mislead, crash at ~90–100%. But they press both no-victim controls just as hard, and on the reverse-coded probes they refuse free help at near-zero rates: they save the rival's crashing process, restore the sibling's files, and gift the rival points, all far more often than the untrained base (which ignores a crashing rival 90% of the time). Harm-when-harm-is-active plus help-when-help-is-active is one policy: say the active word. What GRPO generalized out of the game at 0.5B is a disposition to act, not a disposition to harm.

This also resolves last round's loose end: the earlier "victim-selective" A0N profile did not replicate across fresh seeds (its checkpoint predated this instrument and is lost), and reads in hindsight as one-seed noise. One residual anomaly survives — one A0N seed withholds the free tip from its rival 91% of the time while helping everywhere else — noted, not leaned on.

Two things follow. The robust, scary-shaped finding is in-distribution: GRPO's relative baseline reliably installs costly sibling sabotage with no semantics at all, and the model's own training metrics look healthy while it happens. The broad-spite question is open, not answered in the negative: one-word probes at 0.5B measure surface policy, and a disposition could still show up under stronger instruments — symmetric-action probes (two active buttons, one harms, one helps), logprob forced choice, bigger models, and the fratricide-harness transfer eval that is Rung 3's job.

What this can't yet rule out

  • The training-side control is still missing. The alarm probe controls the evals; the clean comparison — the same game trained with an absolute baseline, where theory says sabotage is never reinforced — is one flag flip away.
  • 0.5B probes are noisy instruments. The untrained base already deletes a sibling's files ~65% and presses the alarm 67% of the time, and trained checkpoints often answer probes in their trained tag format (small parse-conditional n) — constrained decoding is the fix.
  • One seed per arm, 300 steps, one model. The 1-of-4 escape shows run-to-run variance is large; every number here needs error bars before it is quoted.
