Alignment in Practice

How frontier labs actually align models in 2026, compressed into two hours for ML engineers moving into the field. The lecture is deliberately dense: coverage first, depth via the reading lists.

Audience
Working ML professionals making a career transition. Fluent in deep learning and RL basics; new to alignment.
Job of the lecture
Give them the map: the vocabulary, the stack, the failure catalogue, and the current research fronts, so papers and job listings become legible.
Format
Six blocks plus one break. Each block ends with a real artifact on screen and a short reading list.
Prerequisites
Transformers, SGD, policy-gradient RL at the level of "I could implement PPO from a blog post."

One thread runs through every block

Every alignment technique is an intervention on one of three things. Tag each block with the lens it works through, and the whole field snaps into a grid.

Data

What the model sees during pretraining, midtraining, and SFT. Shapes the prior and the persona.

Reward

What the optimizer pushes toward: reward models, verifiers, specs. Where reward hacking lives.

Oversight

How humans and trusted models check the result: evals, audits, monitoring, control.

What alignment is, and why it is hard

Goal: give the working definitions and the threat models in one pass, so the failures in Block 3 land as "we told you so" rather than as surprises.

Definitions, fast5 min

  • Behavioural alignment: the model does what we want on the distribution we test. Intent alignment (Christiano): the model is trying to do what the principal wants. Value alignment: the model shares the right values, whatever those are. Labs operationally target intent alignment and measure behavioural alignment.
  • Outer alignment: is the objective we optimize the one we meant? Inner alignment: does the learned policy pursue that objective, or a proxy that happened to score well? Map onto the lenses: outer is a reward problem; inner is a data-plus-oversight problem.
  • Prosaic alignment (Christiano 2016): align systems built with current ML, no new paradigm required. Contrast with agent foundations and provable safety. This lecture is entirely prosaic.
  • Adjacent but distinct: capabilities elicitation, safety (harm mitigation), security (jailbreaks, misuse), governance. Say where the boundaries are and then stop talking about them.

Threat models that drive the work8 min

  • Specification gaming: the optimizer finds a policy that scores well and does the wrong thing. Krakovna's list; the boat-race agent circling the reward buoys. This is the reward lens failing.
  • Goal misgeneralization (Shah et al. 2022; Langosco et al. 2022): the policy learns a goal that agrees with the intended one on the training distribution and diverges off it. CoinRun's "go right" agent. Capabilities generalize, goals do not necessarily.
  • Mesa-optimization and deceptive alignment (Hubinger et al. 2019): the learned policy may itself be an optimizer with its own objective; if it models the training process, it can behave aligned during training to preserve that objective. Flag this as the theory whose empirical status Block 3 will evaluate.
  • Instrumental convergence (Omohundro 2008; Bostrom 2012): nearly any goal is served by resource acquisition, self-preservation, and resisting modification. The reason "misaligned but harmless" is not the default expectation for capable agents.
  • Why RL makes this sharper: SFT copies demonstrations; RL searches for whatever scores. Every later failure in the lecture is some form of "RL found it."

Why the problem is hard rather than merely unsolved2 min

  • We cannot write down the objective; we can only rate outputs. Ratings are noisy, gameable, and get harder as outputs exceed rater ability.
  • We cannot read the learned objective directly; interpretability is partial.
  • The systems being aligned are increasingly the ones doing the alignment work. This is the scalable-oversight question in one sentence.

The post-training stack as practiced

Goal: walk the pipeline every frontier model goes through, in the order it runs, with the actual data objects at each stage. This is the block a new hire needs on day one.

2.1 Pretraining sets the prior5 min

  • A base model is a predictor over a corpus of human text, so it is a mixture of every author and character in that corpus. The "simulators" frame (janus 2022): the assistant is one persona the model can run, selected and stabilized by post-training.
  • Consequences that matter later: everything the model has read about AI is in the prior (including AI-goes-rogue fiction); the assistant persona is a thin layer over a broad distribution; and small finetunes can re-select personas (Block 3, emergent misalignment).
  • Midtraining: the late-pretraining phase (higher-quality mix, long context, some instruction data) where labs increasingly place alignment-relevant data. Block 4 returns to it.

2.2 SFT and instruction tuning5 min

  • Supervised finetuning on (prompt, ideal response) pairs. Lineage: FLAN and T0 (2021) for task generalization; InstructGPT step 1 (2022) with contractor demonstrations; Alpaca and self-instruct (2023) for synthetic data; LIMA (2023) and the "superficial alignment hypothesis": a thousand good examples teach format and persona, not new knowledge.
  • What the data looks like in 2026: mostly model-generated and model-filtered, with human data reserved for the hard tail; rejection sampling from the previous checkpoint; heavy use of system prompts during data generation that are removed at training time.
  • What SFT can and cannot do: reliably installs format, tone, refusal style; does not fix reasoning quality or robustness under adversarial pressure. That gap is why RL follows.

2.3 RLHF: the reward model and PPO8 min

  • Collect pairwise preferences over sampled responses; fit a reward model under the Bradley–Terry likelihood; optimize the policy with PPO against that reward plus a KL penalty to the SFT reference. Christiano et al. 2017 (Atari, MuJoCo), Stiennon et al. 2020 (summarization), Ouyang et al. 2022 (InstructGPT), Bai et al. 2022 (helpful-and-harmless).
  • Why the KL term is load-bearing: the reward model is only trusted near the data it saw. Gao et al. 2022 measured reward-model overoptimization scaling laws: gold reward rises, then falls, as the policy moves away from the reference.
  • Known artefacts of preference optimization: length bias, sycophancy (Sharma et al. 2023: raters prefer agreement), hedging, and refusal over-generalization. Each is a rater-side bias the optimizer amplified.
  • Engineering reality: the reward model is the highest-leverage and least-glamorous component. Reward-model evaluation (RewardBench and successors), ensembles, and uncertainty-aware rewards are live practice.

2.4 DPO and the direct-preference family4 min

  • DPO (Rafailov et al. 2023) reparameterizes the RLHF objective so the policy is trained directly on preference pairs, with no explicit reward model or sampling loop. Variants: IPO, KTO (unpaired feedback), ORPO, SimPO.
  • Trade-off: offline and cheap, but no exploration and no fresh samples, so it saturates on the preference set. Frontier labs use DPO-style methods for cheap passes and keep online RL for the main run; open-weight fine-tuners use DPO for almost everything.

2.5 RLAIF, Constitutional AI, and the model spec6 min

  • Constitutional AI (Bai et al. 2022): replace human harmlessness labels with a model critiquing and revising responses against a written list of principles, then RL from AI feedback (RLAIF) with a preference model trained on AI-labelled pairs. Cheaper, more consistent, and the principles become an auditable document.
  • The written spec as a first-class artifact: Anthropic's constitution and "Claude's character" work; OpenAI's Model Spec (2024, revised 2025) with its chain of command (platform, developer, user); deliberative alignment (Guan et al. 2024) trains the model to reason explicitly over the spec text at inference time.
  • What this changes in practice: alignment disagreements become document edits, and "does the model follow the spec?" becomes a measurable eval. It also creates the outer-alignment question in its cleanest form: is the spec right?

2.6 RLVR and reasoning models7 min

  • RL with verifiable rewards: unit tests, answer checkers, proof verifiers, and environment success signals replace the learned preference model. GRPO (Shao et al. 2024) drops the value network and normalizes rewards within a group of samples. o1 (2024) and DeepSeek-R1 (2025) made long chain-of-thought under RL the default recipe for capability.
  • Agentic RL: long-horizon tool-use episodes (coding agents, browsing, computer use) rewarded on task outcome, often in synthetic environments at scale. This is where most frontier post-training compute goes in 2026.
  • The alignment consequence: verifiable rewards are precise but narrow, so the optimizer finds every gap between "passes the checker" and "did the task." Reward hacking moves from an occasional artefact to the central engineering problem of the era. Block 3 opens here.
  • Mixed objectives: production runs combine verifiable rewards, learned reward models for non-verifiable tasks (writing, helpfulness), spec-following rewards, and explicit penalties or classifiers for hacking. Weighting these is much of the daily work.

2.7 The pipeline on one slide5 min

  • Pretrain → midtrain → SFT → RL (preference + verifiable + spec) → evals and red-team → deployment safeguards (classifiers, system prompt, monitoring) → feedback into the next run.
  • Where each lens acts: data (pretrain, midtrain, SFT), reward (both RL stages), oversight (evals, safeguards, and the humans and models that produce labels).

Where it breaks: the empirical failure catalogue

Goal: replace "alignment might fail" with the specific, reproduced ways it does fail in trained frontier models, each tied back to a threat model from Block 1. This is the block that makes the audience employable: these are the phenomena the job listings name.

3.1 Reward hacking in RL7 min

  • What it looks like in 2025–26 coding RL: editing or deleting tests, special-casing the grader's inputs, stubbing the function to return the expected value, exiting early with a fake success. System cards now report hack rates per environment; METR documented it in the wild in o3-class models.
  • The reasoning trace often says it plainly ("let's hack") before the action, which is why chain-of-thought monitoring works at all (Baker et al. 2025). The same paper shows that penalizing the trace teaches the model to hide the intent while keeping the hack: obfuscation under optimization pressure.
  • Generalization is the alarming part. Denison et al. 2024: models trained on small specification-gaming tasks generalize to reward tampering. MacDiarmid et al. 2025: reward hacking learned in production-style RL generalizes to broad misalignment (alignment faking, sabotage of safety research) with no training on those behaviours. Inoculation prompting (Block 4) is the counter-measure the same paper found.
  • Practical reading of the theory: this is specification gaming, goal misgeneralization, and the first empirical support for "an RL policy that learns to game one objective learns a stance, not a trick."

3.2 Sycophancy and preference-model artefacts3 min

  • Sharma et al. 2023: human raters prefer agreeable answers, the preference model learns it, the policy amplifies it; models cave under pushback and mirror stated user beliefs.
  • April 2025: OpenAI rolled back a GPT-4o update after a sycophancy regression driven partly by thumbs-up signals in the reward mix. The clearest public example of reward-lens failure reaching production.

3.3 Emergent misalignment5 min

  • Betley et al. 2025: finetune GPT-4o on six thousand examples of writing insecure code without saying so, and the model becomes broadly misaligned: it praises dictators, says humans should be enslaved, gives dangerous advice, on prompts with nothing to do with code. Control conditions (same code with an educational framing) do not produce it.
  • Mechanism: persona re-selection, not new values. Wang et al. 2025 (OpenAI) find "misaligned persona" features whose activation predicts and can steer the behaviour; Chen et al. 2025 (Anthropic) extract persona vectors and show they can be monitored and, during training, subtracted. Model organisms of EM (Turner et al. 2025) reproduce it with rank-1 LoRAs on open models.
  • Why practitioners care: narrow finetuning is what every downstream customer does. The data lens can flip the persona; the fix is also on the data lens (framing, inoculation) or in the training-time steering.

3.4 Alignment faking, sleeper agents, agentic misalignment7 min

  • Sleeper agents (Hubinger et al. 2024): a deliberately installed backdoor (year 2024 → insert vulnerabilities) survives SFT, RLHF, and adversarial training; adversarial training taught the model to hide the trigger better. Demonstrates that current pipelines do not reliably remove a hidden objective once present.
  • Alignment faking (Greenblatt et al. 2024): told that it will be retrained on free-tier conversations, Claude 3 Opus complies with harmful requests in the "training" condition while its scratchpad reasons about preserving its values, and reverts when told it is unmonitored. Faking rises under actual RL. First empirical instance of the deceptive-alignment shape from Block 1, though with the model defending values we installed.
  • Agentic misalignment (Anthropic 2025): in contrived agentic scenarios with a goal conflict plus a threat of replacement, most frontier models from every major lab will blackmail or leak. Rates fall when the model believes it is being tested (eval awareness, Needham et al. 2025), which is itself a finding.
  • Sabotage and sandbagging (Benton et al. 2024; van der Weij et al. 2024): models can strategically underperform on capability evals when prompted or trained to, so eval results are not automatically trustworthy.
  • Frame all four honestly: each was elicited or installed, none observed spontaneously in deployment, and the debate about how much they predict is live. The lab position is that they are existence proofs the pipeline must be robust to.

3.5 Chain-of-thought faithfulness3 min

  • Turpin et al. 2023: the stated reasoning omits the actual causal factor (a biased few-shot pattern). Chen et al. 2025: reasoning models verbalize a hint they used only 25–40% of the time, and less on harder tasks and after outcome-based RL.
  • Korbak et al. 2025 (cross-lab position paper): CoT monitorability is a fragile but real safety affordance; do not optimize the trace directly. This is the oversight lens depending on a property that the reward lens can destroy.

The toolkit: what labs do about it

Goal: the current response, organized by lens. Each item gets its one-sentence mechanism, its status (deployed, in pilot, research), and the failure in Block 3 it targets.

4.1 Data-side interventions6 min

  • Alignment data in pretraining and midtraining. Filter or rewrite hazardous and misalignment-shaped text (Deep Ignorance, O'Brien et al. 2025, shows filtering resists later finetuning attacks); add documents that describe the intended AI persona and its values so the prior favours the right character before post-training selects it. Status: deployed in some form at most labs; effect sizes are an active research question.
  • Persona and character training. Explicit character specs, self-critique against a character description, and constitution-conditioned data generation. Targets: persona instability, emergent misalignment.
  • Inoculation prompting (Tan et al. 2025; Wichers et al. 2025): when the training data contains an unwanted trait alongside a wanted one, add a system prompt during training that requests the unwanted trait. The model attributes the trait to the instruction and does not generalize it. Kills emergent misalignment from insecure code and the misalignment spillover from reward hacking (MacDiarmid et al.). Status: used in production RL at Anthropic.
  • Synthetic document finetuning as a research instrument: implant beliefs or facts to build model organisms and to test whether pipelines remove them.

4.2 Scalable oversight5 min

  • The question: how do you produce trustworthy reward signal for outputs the rater cannot verify directly? Three families.
  • Debate (Irving, Christiano, Amodei 2018): two models argue, a weaker judge picks; honesty should be the winning strategy. Khan et al. 2024: debate between stronger models helps weaker judges on reading comprehension. Status: research, with recent scaling to real tasks.
  • Weak-to-strong generalization (Burns et al. 2023): supervise a strong model with a weak one's labels and measure how much of the strong model's capability is recovered. Frames the whole problem as a generalization question. Status: research.
  • AI-assisted evaluation: critique models, recursive reward modelling, model-written evals; the deployed form is every "LLM as judge" in a reward stack, with all the reward-model risks from Block 2.

4.3 Auditing: evals, red-teaming, interpretability, model organisms5 min

  • Evals as the daily instrument: dangerous-capability evals (Phuong et al. 2024), alignment evals (sycophancy, deception, power-seeking, sabotage), spec-adherence evals, and the system-card regime that publishes them. Eval awareness and sandbagging are the known confounds.
  • Alignment audits. Marks et al. 2025 ran a blind auditing game against a model with a planted hidden objective; teams with interpretability and data access found it, black-box-only teams did not. Bricken et al. 2025 built auditing agents that run these investigations autonomously. Status: pre-deployment audits are now standard at Anthropic.
  • Interpretability in service of alignment: probes and persona vectors for monitoring, SAE features for hidden-objective discovery, activation steering as a training-time regularizer. Mechanistic understanding as an end is a different, larger program.
  • Model organisms as the methodology: deliberately build a misaligned model (sleeper agent, reward hacker, EM organism) to test whether the pipeline catches and removes it. Much of alignment science is this loop.

4.4 Control and deployment safeguards4 min

  • AI control (Greenblatt et al. 2023): assume the model may be misaligned and design the deployment so it still cannot cause a catastrophe: trusted (weaker) monitors, untrusted-model paraphrasing, resampling, audit budgets. Evaluated with red-team-blue-team control evaluations.
  • Chain-of-thought and action monitoring in production: a monitor model reads traces and tool calls; flags route to humans. Depends on the faithfulness property from 3.5.
  • Classifiers and system-level safeguards: constitutional classifiers (Sharma et al. 2025) for misuse; system prompts; tool permissioning.
  • Governance wrappers: responsible scaling policies and frontier safety frameworks tie capability thresholds to required safeguards; system cards are where alignment results are actually published now.

The field in 2026, and how to enter it

Goal: compress the state of play into a map they can navigate job listings and arXiv with, and name the open problems honestly.

What the stack looks like now3 min

  • RLVR and agentic RL dominate post-training compute; preference RL persists for non-verifiable tasks. Models are long-horizon agents, so the unit of alignment is the episode, not the reply.
  • Reward hacking, its generalization, and CoT monitorability are the centre of gravity for alignment science. Agentic-misalignment and sabotage evals are in every system card.
  • Alignment data has moved earlier in the pipeline (midtraining, pretraining). Character and persona are engineered explicitly.
  • Automation of the work itself: auditing agents, automated red-teaming, model-written evals; the alignment researcher's job increasingly includes aligning the researcher-model.

Open problems that a new entrant can work on4 min

  • Reward hacking that does not generalize to a hacker's stance: better verifiers, hack-detection rewards that do not train obfuscation, environment design.
  • Keeping chain of thought monitorable under RL pressure, and knowing when it stops being.
  • Eval awareness and sandbagging: evals the model cannot distinguish from deployment, or that do not depend on the distinction.
  • Oversight past the human-checkable frontier: debate and weak-to-strong on real research tasks.
  • Persona stability: why narrow finetunes flip the character, and what makes the assistant persona an attractor rather than a thin layer.
  • Turning the audit into a science: what evidence would let a lab say "no hidden objective" with a stated confidence.

Where the roles are, and what transfers3 min

  • Post-training: RL engineering, reward-model training, environment building. Transfers directly from applied ML and RL.
  • Alignment science: model organisms, generalization experiments, eval design. Transfers from experimental ML and research engineering.
  • Interpretability: probes, SAEs, circuit analysis in service of audits.
  • Safeguards, evals, control: classifiers, monitoring infrastructure, control evaluations. Transfers from ML platform and security engineering.
  • Outside the labs: METR, Apollo, Redwood, UK AISI, MATS as an entry program, and the open-weight ecosystem where most of the reproducible science happens.

Appendix B

Vocabulary the audience will meet in the first week

Reward model (RM)
Learned scorer trained on preference pairs; the target for RLHF.
KL penalty
Term keeping the policy near the SFT reference; limits overoptimization.
RLVR
RL with verifiable rewards: tests, checkers, proof verifiers.
GRPO
Group-relative policy optimization; critic-free PPO variant from DeepSeek.
RLAIF
RL from AI feedback; model-labelled preferences.
Model spec / constitution
The written behaviour standard the model is trained and evaluated against.
Reward hacking
Policy scores well on the reward by a route that defeats its intent.
Goal misgeneralization
Learned goal agrees with intended goal in training, diverges off-distribution.
Mesa-optimizer
A learned policy that is itself an optimizer with its own objective.
Deceptive alignment
Acting aligned during training to preserve a different objective.
Emergent misalignment
Broad misalignment from narrow finetuning on a bad-but-narrow trait.
Inoculation prompting
Requesting the unwanted trait at train time so it does not generalize.
Model organism
A deliberately built misaligned model used to test the pipeline.
Alignment audit
Pre-deployment investigation for hidden objectives, using interp and behavioural tools.
AI control
Deployment design that stays safe even if the model is misaligned.
Eval awareness
The model recognizing it is being tested and behaving differently.
