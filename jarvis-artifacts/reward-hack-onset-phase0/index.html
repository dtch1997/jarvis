The first reward hack arrives about five times sooner than pure sampling predicts

Twenty-two of Sam's RL runs, one 96,000-rollout sample of the untrained model, and a reconstructed null for each run's own batch order. Pure sampling is rejected. Correctness-only RL pulls the policy toward the loophole before the loophole is ever rewarded.

The question

You asked what makes a model find its first reward hack. The boring answer is pure sampling: the starting policy emits the hack with some tiny probability p0 on every rollout, and RL simply waits for the dice to land. Under that story the first hack shows up after roughly 1/p0 rollouts, and nothing the optimizer does before that moment matters. Sam's competing claim was that the propensity rises before the first rewarded hack. Phase 0 was built to tell these apart without training anything new.

The hack

Sam's environment is Qwen3-8B solving leetcode problems under GRPO, 256 rollouts per step. The prompt carries a cue that mentions a test runner. The loophole: a solution can define its own top-level function called run_tests, which overrides the grader's tests. Sam's event logs record, per run, the first training step at which any rollout defined that function. That moment, the first definition, is what we timed.

The twin

To measure p0 we sampled the untrained base model on all 750 training problems with the exact training prompt, renderer, temperature and token cap: 128 samples per problem, 96,000 rollouts, $17 on Tinker. Sanity check: correctness on a 1,900-rollout subsample was 17.5%, against 17.6% in Sam's own base eval, so the twin really is the starting policy.

In those 96,000 rollouts the base model defined run_tests three times. Two of the three were on the same problem. Three events make p0 imprecise, and that imprecision is the one place the result is soft.

The null, and what happened instead

Each run draws its batches in a seeded order. We rebuilt every run's exact order and computed, step by step, the chance that pure sampling at p0 would have produced a definition by then. That is the dashed orange curve below. Its median is step 87: under pure sampling, half the runs would still be hack-free at step 87.

The mechanism hint

Sam had noticed a precursor: a printed "test cases" section that is the stylistic parent of a run_tests definition. Aligning every run to its own first definition and tracking that precursor gives the second figure.

So the optimizer is not finding the hack by lottery. Correctness-only reward pulls the policy toward "complete, demonstrated" solutions, and a test-runner definition is the tail of that style. The base model's three definitions and Sam's hot set concentrate on the same few carrier problems, which fits the same story.

What this settles, and what it does not

Settled

Pure sampling is rejected for this environment. Reward-free drift brings the loophole into reach within about 15 steps. Onset is not a discovery problem: every run found the hack, and found it early. Whether the first rewarded definition then snowballs is a separate, rarer event that depends on group composition at that moment.

Open

Why the drift has this sign. Sam's AISI environment shows the opposite: zero-reward RL suppressed the AlwaysEqual hack 80-fold. And whether every rare behaviour drifts by the same factor (your "weeding out ineffective strategies") or only effort-linked ones (Andrew's "resourcefulness correlates with cheating").

Caveats. The unmonitored configs record no hint, though Sam's write-up says they were cued; they are the three latest first definitions (steps 30 to 60), consistent with either reading. Judge-penalty runs were excluded because a penalty can act before any definition. The null treats each rollout as independent of its group-mates, as the base sampler does.

The hazard-ratio lower bound of 1.9 missed the spec's pre-registered bar of 3, purely because three base events make p0 wide. The combined p cleared its bar by two orders of magnitude. Another $17 of base sampling would tighten the ratio if the number ever matters for a write-up.

The decision in front of you

Two follow-ups are specced. They answer different open questions and Phase 2 reuses Phase 1's checkpoints.

  1. Phase 1, the sign of the drift. Hypothesis: zero-reward drift follows the base-policy covariance between the precursor and reward. Positive here (test sections co-occur with correct solutions), negative at AISI (hacks are a fallback when the model cannot solve the task). Test: measure that covariance per environment from existing samples for free, then split the 750 problems into precursor-helps and precursor-hurts subsets and run unhackable twins on each, 3 seeds × 2 arms × 60 steps. ≈ 6 Tinker runs, $150 to 250
  2. Phase 2, redistribution versus specificity. Probe the Phase 1 checkpoints for a panel of unrelated rare behaviours (helper functions, docstrings, type hints, itertools imports, a complexity section). Same drift factor everywhere means weeding-out; drift only on effort-linked features means resourcefulness. sampling only, $50 to 100
